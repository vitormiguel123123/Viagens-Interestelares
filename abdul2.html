ABOUT OUR TOPIC


Interstellar travel involves spacecraft traveling from one star to another in the universe, which could help us discover planets that may harbor forms of life. These journeys would also help us learn more about stars, planets, and solar systems, and could eventually be used by humanity to colonize a new solar system.
The technology needed to make this possible is essential. Some of the most crucial factors to consider are vast distances and the amount of time these journeys would take. Improvements in propulsion technology could reduce the time these journeys take. In the future, robots and probes will likely be used first to avoid putting human lives at risk. Interstellar travel will greatly expand our horizons, allowing us to understand the universe far better than ever before.



THE BEGINNING OF SPACE EXPLORATION

I. THE END OF WORLD WAR II



Germany Is Defeated

◆︎ After World War II ended, the territories belonging to Nazi Germany were divided between the United States and its allies, and the Soviet Union. The occupation led the two victorious powers to discover advanced weapons developed by the Nazis and abandoned after their defeat. One of them was the revolutionary V-2 (Vergeltungswaffe 2), or Vengeance Weapon 2, which used a fuel mixture of ethanol and liquid oxygen. Rocket tests conducted by both nations saw the rocket reach an altitude of 175 km, making it the first human-made object to cross the Kármán line and reach space, though it failed to achieve orbit.





Using plans for advanced Nazi technology, both countries began creating their own designs inspired by the V-2, with the intention of outdoing one another in space technology—a field also influenced by the development of intercontinental missiles, which would prove crucial to the Space Race.
During the 1940s and 1950s, the United States developed the Viking sounding rocket, which provided valuable scientific data about the boundaries of space between 1949 and 1955. Meanwhile, the Soviets developed the R-1, a tactical ballistic missile based on the German rocket design. It was never an effective strategic weapon; however, the production and launch of the R-1 provided the Soviets with valuable experience that later enabled the USSR to build its own rockets—rockets that were far more capable.



II. SPUTNIK AND SOVIET DOMINANCE



The Sputnik, rocket that launched the first satellite into space, also named Sputnik.

The Soviets Take the Lead

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union, adapted one of its intercontinental missiles R-7 Semyorka for its space program, launched the first satellite artificial device to reach orbit, Sputnik-1 (Russian for “Satellite-1”). It was a polished metal sphere with four external radio antennas that transmitted radio pulses.
With this first leap, the Soviets moved one step ahead of the Americans, sending the first animal into space, Laika aboard the Sputnik 2 and then, in 1961, the first human into space with Vostok-1 crewed by Yuri Gagarin.

Laika, the first animal in space, and Yuri Gagarin, the first human to orbit Earth.



III. AMERICA RECLAIMS THE PODIUM



From left to right: Astronaut Buzz Aldrin steps onto the lunar surface during the Apollo 11 mission; Command and Support Module of the Apollo program, which brought the astronauts to the Moon.

Following the significant advances made by the Soviets, the United States, led by John F. Kennedy, began its own space initiatives, driven by the government. Starting with simple, robotic rockets, American technology advanced significantly over the years, sending the first American, Alan Shepard, into space on the Mercury-Redstone 3 mission. The Americans made further progress with the Gemini Program, which envisioned more spaceflights and even the docking of spacecraft in orbit; the program was a success, providing the United States with valuable experience that would define its impact on history.
Between 1961 and 1969, the Apollo Program sent humans into space aboard the Saturn V rocket, beginning with Apollo 7 in 1968. This was followed by the first humans to reach the Moon in 1969 (via the Apollo 8 mission), and, ultimately, the first humans to walk on the Moon in 1969: Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin..
