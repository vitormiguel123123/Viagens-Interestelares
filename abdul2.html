ABOUT OUR TOPIC


Interstellar travel involves spacecraft traveling from one star to another within the universe, potentially helping us discover planets that may harbor life. These journeys would also help us learn more about new stars, planets, and solar systems, and could eventually allow humanity to colonize another solar system. The technology required to make this possible is essential. One of the most crucial factors to consider is the vast distances involved and the time these journeys would take. Improvements in propulsion technology could help address the lengthy duration of such trips. In the future, robots and probes will likely be used first, before humans are put at risk. Therefore, interstellar travel could greatly expand our horizons, allowing us to explore and understand the universe better than ever before.



THE BEGINNING OF SPACE EXPLORATION

I. THE END OF WORLD WAR II



Germany Is Defeated

◆ After the end of World War II, the territories that had belonged to Nazi Germany were divided between the United States and its allies, and the Soviet Union. During the occupation, the two victorious powers discovered several advanced technological weapons developed by the Nazis and abandoned after their defeat. One of them was the revolutionary V-2 (Vergeltungswaffe 2), or "Vengeance Weapon 2," which used a propellant consisting of ethanol and liquid oxygen. Rocket tests conducted by both nations allowed the V-2 to reach an altitude of 175 km, making it the first human-made object to cross the Kármán line and reach space, although it did not achieve orbit.





Using the blueprints for advanced Nazi technology, both countries began developing their own designs inspired by the V-2, seeking to surpass one another in space technology. This competition was also influenced by the development of intercontinental ballistic missiles, which would become crucial to the Space Race.

During the late 1940s and early 1950s, the United States developed the Viking sounding rocket, which provided valuable scientific data about the upper atmosphere and the boundaries of space between 1949 and 1955. Meanwhile, the Soviets developed the R-1, a tactical ballistic missile based on the design of the German rocket. It was never an effective strategic weapon. However, the production and launch of the R-1 provided the Soviets with valuable experience that later enabled the USSR to build its own, much more capable rockets.
